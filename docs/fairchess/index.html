    Fair Chess

    Chess without a first move: both players choose a move in secret, then both are revealed and made at once. A move goes through only if it's legal whichever went first, so two pieces bound for one square stop each other, and a piece that runs holds its captor. Capture the king to win. From the post Fair Chess and Simultaneous Games.

    The examples from the post, to resolve step by step. Every piece is its own player: click any piece, then a square, to change what it requests; click it twice to take its request back.

      How it works

      Each round, both players request a move: any move a piece could make, as if it were their turn. There's no check; a king is lost by being captured, and castling is allowed through attack. Requests are revealed together and merged:

      A move is made only if it is legal in both orders, White's first or Black's. If one is, it's made, and the other is tried again on the new board.

      • Collision. Both to one empty square: whichever went first would turn the other into a capture, a different move. Neither moves.
      • Block. A piece lands in another's path: it moves, the other doesn't.
      • Escape. A piece runs as it's captured: it can't run if taken first, and the capture misses if it ran first. Neither moves, so a king that moves can't be taken that round.

      With more players (in the figures, every piece is one) it's a loop: make the moves legal in every ordering of those still undecided, block what that made illegal, and go again until nothing changes. What's left is held. The trace beside the board shows each step. It works for any turn-based game, not just chess.

      A position three times, or fifty rounds without a capture or a pawn move, is a draw. From Fair Chess and Simultaneous Games.
